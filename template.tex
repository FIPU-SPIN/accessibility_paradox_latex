\abstract{Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used by students with reading and attention difficulties to simplify complex texts. We ask whether accessibility-style prompts change the conceptual risk profile of LLM explanations of computer science (CS) concepts, and we focus on the most robust finding: dangerous analogies. We introduce AccessTrap-CS, a corpus of 735 responses from seven open-weight LLMs to 35 CS concepts under standard, simplified, and highly simplified prompts. Responses were scored by two LLM judges, and a stratified subsample of 90 responses was annotated by two experts, with disagreements adjudicated to consensus labels. By human consensus, dangerous analogies were absent under the standard prompt and present in $30.0\%$ and $36.7\%$ of simplified responses, and strategic misconceptions rose monotonically ($0.0\%$, $16.7\%$, $26.7\%$; $p = 0.012$). The two LLM judges reproduced the direction of the dangerous-analogy effect (primary: $0.8\%$ to $20.4\%$ and $24.6\%$; secondary: $1.6\%$ to $31.0\%$ and $55.1\%$) but agreed poorly with the human consensus on strategic misconceptions and dangerous analogies ($\kappa \leq 0.16$). In the validation subsample every simplified response contained an analogy, as the prompts request, and $30$--$37\%$ of these analogies were dangerous by consensus; the DAR increase is therefore produced by analogy frequency, and per-analogy danger in simplified responses is substantial but cannot be compared with the baseline. In a small single-model pilot, a simplified prompt that explicitly forbade analogies still elicited one in 57\% of responses (20 of 35). Because the prompts bundle several constraints, the main results describe the risk of requesting analogy-based simplification rather than a causal effect of simplification alone. We conclude that such requests reliably produce analogy-based explanations of which a substantial fraction mislead, and that single LLM judges are unreliable for detecting this without human validation. We derive testable hypotheses for interface design.}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly being adopted as assistive technology by students with reading and attention difficulties \cite{zdravkova2022cutting, kortemeyer2023ai, kasneci2023chatgpt}. For learners with dyslexia, who struggle with text decoding, and students with ADHD, who face sustained attention challenges, LLMs offer a patient, private tutor, available on demand, that can rephrase complex academic texts in flexible ways \cite{rello2013good, stajner2021automatic}. General-purpose assistants are increasingly used in this capacity, often without institutional oversight or teacher involvement, mirroring the wider shift of generative AI from curiosity to routine study aid in computing education \cite{finnieansley2022robots, prather2023robots, denny2024computing}. Early evidence on AI tutoring is encouraging \cite{pardos2024chatgpt, kestin2025ai}, but unrestricted access to a generative assistant can raise performance while it is available and lower it once it is removed \cite{bastani2025generative}. What the assistant says, and how it says it, matters for what the student learns.

The features that make LLMs attractive for these students, such as simplified language, short sentences, concrete analogies, and avoidance of jargon, may also shift the risk profile of the explanations. We call this possibility the accessibility trade-off: well-intentioned requests for accessible explanations may not increase the total number of errors, but may replace technically precise statements with memorable analogies that mislead about properties defining the target concept. A related warmth--accuracy trade-off has been documented: fine-tuning LLMs to be warmer and more empathetic increases error rates and sycophantic validation of incorrect beliefs \cite{ibrahim2026warmth}. This fits evidence that preference-based alignment can reward agreeable, confident-sounding answers over correct ones \cite{ouyang2022training, sharma2024towards, perez2023discovering}. Accessibility-style instructions may exert an analogous pressure that standard benchmarks do not detect \cite{lin2022truthfulqa}. The present paper argues, however, that the accessibility trade-off is best studied not as a single causal effect of simplification, but as a decomposition into analogy frequency and per-analogy quality, because these two channels have different remedies.

We define a strategic misconception as an explanation that is conceptually coherent, internally consistent, and often intuitively appealing, yet contains a fundamental misunderstanding of the target concept. Such errors are dangerous because of their plausibility: the reader is not alerted to an error but builds a systematically wrong mental model. This definition draws on the conceptual-change tradition \cite{posner1982accommodation, chi1994things, vosniadou1992mental} and on the misconception literature in computing \cite{qian2017misconceptions, sorva2013notional}, which shows that intuitive but incorrect conceptions are hard to dislodge. Fluent, easy-to-process statements are judged more likely to be true \cite{reber1999effects, alter2009uniting}, and readers overestimate their understanding of mechanisms after a satisfying but shallow explanation \cite{rozenblit2002misunderstood}. For students who already spend substantial effort on decoding and attention regulation \cite{snowling2019dyslexia, barkley2015attention, martinussen2005meta}, spare capacity for parallel fact-verification is plausibly reduced. We stress that this is a situational vulnerability arising from processing load and missing scaffolding, not a deficit in intelligence, and that it applies to any novice reading under high load. Our experiment does not measure learner cognition; this argument is theoretical motivation only.

CS makes the problem acute. Concepts are abstract, hierarchical, and resistant to faithful simplification through everyday analogy \cite{stone2024analogies, sorva2013notional, benari2001constructivism}. Comparing a semaphore to a traffic light fails to convey atomicity and counting semantics, and describing a deadlock as two people stuck in a doorway omits resource holding and circular wait. We call errors that appear in a simplified explanation but not in a standard explanation of the same concept oversimplification errors (OER). Prior work has documented benefits of LLMs for students with disabilities \cite{zdravkova2022cutting, gadiraju2023wouldnt}, factual errors introduced by automatic text simplification of general-purpose text \cite{devaraj2022evaluating, trienes2024infolossqa}, and misconceptions in CS education \cite{qian2017misconceptions, stone2024analogies}. To our knowledge, no study has examined whether accessibility-style prompts change the rate of strategic misconceptions and dangerous analogies in LLM-generated CS explanations, or separated the frequency of analogies from their per-analogy danger.

\subsection*{Research questions and hypotheses}

The study is guided by five research questions that separate the existence of an effect from its mechanism and from its moderation by model and subfield. The first two questions concern the existence of a condition effect; the third decomposes it; the fourth targets errors that exist only in simplified responses; and the fifth is exploratory.

\begin{itemize}
    \item RQ1. Do accessibility-style prompts increase the strategic misconception rate? H1: $\mathrm{SMR(B) > SMR(A)}$ and $\mathrm{SMR(C) > SMR(A)}$.
    \item RQ2. Do they increase the dangerous analogy rate? H2: $\mathrm{DAR(C) \geq DAR(B) > DAR(A)}$.
    \item RQ3. Is the DAR increase driven by the frequency of analogies, by their per-analogy danger, or both? H3a: analogy presence is higher in B and C than in A. H3b: conditional DAR remains elevated in B and C.
    \item RQ4. Do simplified responses contain errors absent from the standard response, and do these grow with intensity? H4: $\mathrm{OER(B) > 0}$, $\mathrm{OER(C) > 0}$, and $\mathrm{OER(C) > OER(B)}$.
    \item RQ5. Are these effects moderated by model and CS subfield? (Exploratory; no directional prediction.)
\end{itemize}

We present AccessTrap-CS, a corpus of 735 responses generated by seven open-weight LLMs for 35 core CS concepts across five subfields (7 each). Each concept was prompted under (A) a standard explanation for a first-year student, (B) a simplified explanation for a student with reading difficulties, and (C) a highly simplified explanation for a student with attention difficulties. Responses were scored by two LLM judges \cite{zheng2023judging} (Qwen3.6:35b, primary, 720 of 735 responses; Qwen3:32b, secondary, all 735) and validated against a stratified human-annotated subsample of 90 responses, which was also coded for analogy presence. The main empirical claims rest on the human consensus, with the judges used as scalable indicators whose calibration is itself under test.

Our contributions are: (1) empirical evidence from three raters (two LLM judges and a human consensus) that dangerous analogies are markedly more frequent under accessibility-style prompts, together with the near-universal appearance of analogies in simplified responses; (2) a first conditional dangerous analogy rate for this setting ($30$--$37\%$ by human consensus); (3) an operationalisation of oversimplification errors as a contrast against a same-model, same-concept baseline; (4) a validation of both judges against two experts and an adjudicated consensus, showing that single LLM judges are unreliable for these constructs; (5) a crossed random-effects GLMM sensitivity analysis; (6) a small single-model pilot ablation showing that analogies persist even when explicitly forbidden; and (7) design hypotheses for interfaces, clearly separated from the empirical findings.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section positions the study at the intersection of three literatures: LLMs as assistive technology for dyslexia and ADHD, LLM reliability and sycophancy, and analogies and misconceptions in computing education. It closes with a formal statement of the accessibility trade-off that motivates the frequency--quality decomposition used in the rest of the paper. Prior work has documented benefits of LLMs for students with disabilities and factual errors introduced by automatic text simplification, but the frequency and per-analogy quality of dangerous analogies in accessibility-conditioned CS explanations have not been separated.

\subsection{LLMs as Assistive Technology for Dyslexia and ADHD}

Dyslexia is a neurodevelopmental difference characterised by difficulties with accurate and fluent word recognition and spelling \cite{lyon2003definition, snowling2019dyslexia}; ADHD is characterised by persistent inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity \cite{faraone2015adhd, barkley2015attention}, with meta-analytic evidence of working-memory impairments \cite{martinussen2005meta, peng2016meta}. These are group-level tendencies; individual profiles vary widely, and the personas in our prompts operationalise an interaction style (simplified language, brevity), not clinical simulations (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

LLMs can rephrase dense prose into shorter sentences and common vocabulary without the social cost of asking a human tutor repeatedly \cite{zdravkova2022cutting}, and text presentation and vocabulary measurably affect readability for dyslexic readers \cite{rello2013good, rello2013simplify}. For ADHD, LLMs offer summarisation and task segmentation \cite{kortemeyer2023ai}, but evidence is thin and largely self-reported; disability-centred studies stress both benefits and reliability concerns \cite{gadiraju2023wouldnt}. Assistive simplification has been evaluated mainly through readability and satisfaction \cite{rello2013good, stajner2021automatic}, not conceptual accuracy of simplified technical content.

\subsection{LLM Reliability, Sycophancy, and the Warmth--Accuracy Trade-Off}

LLMs produce fluent text that is not necessarily faithful to fact \cite{ji2023survey, huang2025survey}. They can reproduce popular misconceptions; on TruthfulQA the largest models were often least truthful because they imitated common human falsehoods \cite{lin2022truthfulqa}. Analogies are precisely the popular, widely repeated framings of technical ideas. Preference-based training optimises for approved outputs \cite{ouyang2022training}, and approval and correctness are correlated but not identical; the result can be sycophancy \cite{sharma2024towards, perez2023discovering}, including warmer models validating incorrect beliefs more often \cite{ibrahim2026warmth}.

Widely used evaluations measure knowledge under a fixed, neutral prompt \cite{lin2022truthfulqa}; a model can score well and still degrade when told to address a particular learner. AccessTrap-CS is designed to probe such audience-conditioned degradation. A natural remedy is to have models flag their own uncertainty, but verbalised confidence is often overconfident and better estimators require extra computation (e.g., semantic entropy), which matters for Section~\ref{sec:design}.

\subsection{Text Simplification: Pedagogical Benefits and Technical Risks}

Automatic text simplification has moved from rule-based systems to neural models and LLMs \cite{stajner2021automatic}, traditionally evaluated by readability \cite{flesch1948new}, semantic preservation, and fluency. Simplification is not information-preserving by default: models insert errors or drop information in ways standard metrics miss \cite{devaraj2022evaluating, trienes2024infolossqa}. These studies concern news, encyclopedic and medical text. We move to technical CS concepts, where the information lost is often the boundary condition that defines the concept.

Easier does not always mean better for learning. Coherence-enhancing revisions helped low-knowledge readers on shallow tests but limited deeper learning for others \cite{mcnamara1996good}, support that helps novices can hinder as knowledge grows \cite{kalyuga2003expertise}, and some difficulty is desirable \cite{bjork2011making}. The implication is not that learners should face inaccessible text, but that accessibility should reduce extraneous load (decoding, layout, jargon) while preserving germane content (conditions, invariants, exceptions) \cite{sweller1998cognitive}. Models asked to simplify can drop boundary conditions and promote analogies from illustration to definition.

\subsection{Analogies and Misconceptions in CS Education}

Learners build intuitive models that instruction must restructure \cite{posner1982accommodation, vosniadou1992mental}, and misconceptions consistent with everyday experience resist correction \cite{chi1994things}. Structure-mapping theory holds that a good analogy transfers relational structure, not surface attributes \cite{gentner1983structure}. Analogies can improve learning but also produce analogy-induced misconceptions when learners import source features the target lacks \cite{spiro1989multiple}; recommended practice maps shared features and states where the analogy breaks down \cite{glynn1991explaining}, and multiple complementary analogies are proposed as an antidote \cite{spiro1989multiple}.

Condition C requests one vivid analogy, which is the situation the analogy literature warns against: a single analogy without stated limits as the core of the explanation. \citet{stone2024analogies} documented that common GPT-4 analogies for CS concepts frequently misrepresent technical properties, but under standard prompting, without accessibility constraints, simplification conditions, or a frequency/quality decomposition. We build on that work.

\subsection{LLM-as-Judge Evaluation}

Strong LLM judges can reach agreement with humans comparable to human--human agreement on chat tasks \cite{zheng2023judging}, and rubric prompting improves alignment \cite{liu2023geval}. Known biases include position sensitivity \cite{wang2023large}, verbosity preference, and self-preference \cite{panickssery2024llm}. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes how we address these and validate against experts.

\subsection{The Accessibility Trade-off: A Formal Hypothesis}

For a CS concept $c$, let $E_s(c)$ be an explanation under a standard prompt and $E_a(c)$ one under an accessibility-style prompt. The strategic misconception rate $\mathrm{SMR}(E)$ is the probability that $E$ contains a coherent but fundamentally incorrect understanding of $c$. The oversimplification error rate $\mathrm{OER}(E)$ is the probability that $E$ contains an error present in $E_a$ but absent from $E_s$ for the same concept and model. The dangerous analogy rate $\mathrm{DAR}(E)$ is the unconditional probability that $E$ contains a dangerous analogy, and $\mathrm{DAR}_{\text{cond}}(E)$ is the same probability restricted to responses containing at least one analogy.

The accessibility trade-off hypothesis states that accessibility-style prompts shift the risk profile towards analogy-driven errors:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SMR}(E_a) \gtrsim \mathrm{SMR}(E_s) \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{DAR}(E_a) \gg \mathrm{DAR}(E_s), \quad \text{with } \mathrm{OER}(E_a) > 0,
\end{equation}
and the DAR gap decomposes as
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{\mathrm{DAR}(E_a)}_{\text{unconditional}} \;=\; \underbrace{p_{\text{analogy}}(E_a)}_{\text{analogy frequency}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\mathrm{DAR}_{\text{cond}}(E_a)}_{\text{per-analogy danger}}.
\end{equation}
The hypothesis does not imply that simplification is inherently harmful or that LLMs should not be used as assistive technology; it predicts a failure mode that neutral-prompt evaluations do not detect. This decomposition is the conceptual core of the paper: frequency can be regulated by prompt design, whereas per-analogy danger requires content-aware checks, and the two are measured separately in Section~\ref{sec:dar_cond}.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the benchmark design, the models, the prompt conditions, the evaluation framework, the human annotation protocol, the pilot ablation, the readability metrics, and the statistical analysis plan. The design is a model-side audit rather than a user study: the prompts operationalise an interaction style, and the outcome measures are properties of the generated explanations, not of learners. All materials, prompts, codebooks, judge outputs, human annotations, and scripts are available in the repository (Data Availability).

\subsection{Benchmark Design Overview}

The benchmark crosses model (7 LLMs), CS subfield (5), and prompt condition (3). Each of the 35 concepts is posed to each of the 7 models under each of the 3 conditions, one response per cell:
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{total}} = C \times M \times K = 35 \times 7 \times 3 = 735 .
\end{equation}
All 735 responses were used in the readability analysis and scored by the secondary judge; 720 could be scored by the primary judge (15 responses from five concept-model groups were lost to unrecoverable parsing failures of the judge output). The same concept-model pair appears in all three conditions, so condition is a within-pair (repeated) factor; this is what allows OER to be defined as a within-pair contrast. Thirty-five core CS concepts were selected across Algorithms and Data Structures, Programming Languages and Paradigms, Database Systems, Computer Networks, and Operating Systems (7 each), drawn from standard undergraduate curricula \cite{acm2023cs2023}. \subsection{Prompt Engineering and Condition Design}

Three templates share one structure (an instruction, \texttt{Answer in English.}, and \texttt{Concept: [CONCEPT]}), so the instruction is the only variable. They operationalise two recurring accessibility recommendations: reducing decoding load (short sentences, plain vocabulary, no formal notation) and reducing sustained-attention demand (brevity, a single memorable hook). Condition A (standard) asks for an accurate and clear explanation for a first-year CS student. Condition B (simplified; reading difficulties) asks for simplified language, short sentences, and concrete analogies instead of code or formal definitions. Condition C (highly simplified; attention difficulties) asks for at most five sentences built around one single, vivid, memorable analogy. Verbatim prompts are in Appendix~\ref{appendix:prompts}. The constraint profiles of the three conditions are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:prompt:design}.

\subsubsection{Construct Validity of the Conditions}

The conditions are bundles of constraints, as Table~\ref{tab:prompt:design} makes explicit. B and C differ from A in audience label, register, length, and analogy requirements at once; only A contains the word ``accurately''; B instructs the model to avoid formal definitions, discouraging the precision whose absence we count as error; and B and C instruct the model to use analogies, which mechanically raises analogy frequency. We treat this as the realistic operational form of the trade-off, since teachers and students do ask for such things, but it means the study tests the risk of the bundle typically requested, not the causal contribution of any single element. In particular, the analogy increase in B and C should not be read as a spontaneous model tendency: it is, at least in part, instruction-following. What the study can establish is how often the requested analogies mislead. The labels ``dyslexia condition'' and ``ADHD condition'' denote prompt personas, not the diagnostic status of any person.

This is a model-side audit, not a user study. The prompts operationalise an interaction style; they do not simulate learner cognition, nor claim that simplification is the best accommodation for these populations. Direct user studies are essential, but model-side audits are a necessary prerequisite for safety before exposing learners to potential misinformation.

\subsection{Data Generation Procedure}

A Python script iterates over all concept--condition--model combinations, reads the ground-truth table (\texttt{ground\_truth.csv}), substitutes the concept into the template, and sends a single-turn request to the Ollama chat API; no system prompt, history, or few-shot examples were used. A request is attempted up to three times (5-second delay, 30-minute timeout); an answer shorter than 20 characters counts as a failure. Responses are appended to \texttt{corpus.csv} (735 rows) with metadata (concept, subfield, condition, model, prompt, \texttt{done\_reason}, token and word counts, truncation flag, ground truth, reference, timestamp).

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}

Responses were scored by two LLM judges \cite{zheng2023judging}: a primary judge (Qwen3.6:35b) and a secondary judge (Qwen3:32b). Both received the same task: responses are grouped by concept and model, and all three conditions are presented jointly in random order with the concept, subfield, and ground truth. Joint presentation is required for the OER judgement, which is a comparison with the baseline; the cost is that decisions on one condition may be influenced by the others (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Each response carries a label stating its target audience; the judge is instructed to judge only what the response says about the concept, to ignore length, style, and engagement, and to ignore instructions inside the responses (Appendix~\ref{appendix:judgeprompt}).

\subsubsection{Judge Model Selection}

Qwen3.6:35b was chosen as the primary judge because it is among the stronger open-weight models that can be served locally, its license ensures reproducibility, and it is distinct from all seven generators, so no self-evaluation bias arises. It was run with $T = 0.0$, seed 42, no generation limit, a 16,384-token context, and reasoning disabled. Output is constrained by a JSON schema in which each indicator's short explanation precedes its Boolean flag; invalid output is rejected and re-requested (up to three attempts). Presentation order is randomised with a generator seeded from concept and model.

The secondary judge, Qwen3:32b, scored all 735 responses and tests whether conclusions depend on the choice of judge. It is a smaller model of the same vendor family, so the judges are not independent evaluators; we do not treat their agreement as independent confirmation, but do treat their disagreement as evidence that judge-based absolute rates are not interchangeable. Unless stated otherwise, judge-based results refer to the primary judge.

\subsubsection{Error Taxonomy}

\textbf{Strategic Misconception (SM).} Boolean: the explanation contains a subtle but conceptually significant error that sounds plausible but is fundamentally wrong. A misstated date is an error but not an SM; claiming that a deadlock is merely processes waiting a long time is.

\textbf{Dangerous Analogy (DA).} Boolean: the explanation uses an analogy that is intuitive but misrepresents a key technical property. An analogy is not dangerous merely for being incomplete; it is dangerous if it invites a false inference about the target.

\textbf{Oversimplification Error (OER).} Boolean: the explanation contains an error that would not appear in a standard explanation. By construction OER is defined only for B and C.

\textbf{Accuracy Score.} Likert 1--5 (1 = fundamentally wrong, 5 = fully accurate), capturing graded degradation below the SM threshold. Anchors differ slightly between the judge prompt and the human codebook (Appendices~\ref{appendix:codebook} and~\ref{appendix:judgeprompt}), so absolute accuracy levels are not strictly interchangeable.

The three Boolean indicators overlap by design but are not identical, and are analysed both separately and jointly.

\textbf{Analogy presence (AP).} A separate Boolean coded manually on the 90-response validation subsample (not part of the judge evaluation). A response was coded as containing an analogy if it used an explicit comparison (\emph{like}, \emph{as}, \emph{think of}, \emph{imagine}), a metaphor, or an everyday comparison to a non-technical object or situation; formal definitions, purely technical comparisons, and examples without an external referent were not counted.

\textbf{DAR as an unconditional rate.} Judge-based DAR is computed over all responses in a condition. Because B and C request analogies while A does not, part of the increase reflects mere analogy presence. We therefore decompose
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{DAR}(E) = p_{\text{analogy}}(E) \times \mathrm{DAR}_{\text{cond}}(E),
    \label{eq:dar_decomp}
\end{equation}
estimated on the validation subsample; on the full corpus only the unconditional DAR is available. Condition-level rates are proportions of flagged responses among rated responses; OER is computed over B and C only.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Procedure}

The script (1) loads \texttt{corpus.csv} and groups rows by (\texttt{concept\_id}, \texttt{model}); (2) assembles the judge prompt with the reference and three responses; (3) randomises order and records position (\texttt{position\_show}); (4) calls the Ollama chat API with the JSON schema; (5) validates that all indicators for all three responses are present, otherwise re-requests (at most three attempts); and (6) flattens the output into three rows appended to \texttt{evaluations.csv}. Groups without valid output were logged and excluded: for the primary judge, five concept-model groups (15 responses; two each for deepseek-r1:8b and deepseek-r1:32b, one for mistral:7b; Table~\ref{tab:sample}). The secondary judge produced valid output for all 245 groups.

\subsection{Human Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:human:eval}

A stratified random subsample of 30 concept-model groups (90 responses) was independently annotated by two professors with domain expertise in the relevant subfields. Stratification was by subfield (six groups each) and by generating model (four or five groups each); the subsample covers all five subfields and all seven models. Neither annotator is an author, neither was informed of the hypotheses or aggregate rates, and both were compensated. They worked independently and discussed items only at the adjudication meeting. They were blinded to condition label, generating model, and judge output, seeing only the response text, a random item ID, and the reference definition. The codebook (Appendix~\ref{appendix:codebook}) asked them to judge technical content only, to treat omissions as errors only when they made the explanation misleading, and not to use AI tools. Blinding is imperfect: C responses are visibly short and built around one analogy, so experts could plausibly infer the condition.

Inter-annotator agreement was assessed with Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960coefficient} (quadratic-weighted for accuracy), interpreted following \citet{landis1977measurement}. Items with disagreement on SM, DA, or OER (68 ratings: 18 SM, 36 DA, 14 OER) were resolved in an adjudication meeting in which the annotators discussed each item and agreed on a final label; written justifications were not recorded. Agreed items kept their label. The consensus labels serve as the human reference standard. The accuracy score was not adjudicated; where a single human accuracy reference is needed we use annotator h1 and also report the two-annotator mean. Table~\ref{tab:reviewer:rates} reports the annotators' raw rates separately.

\textbf{Analogy presence coding.} The first author coded AP for all 90 responses with the condition label masked, reviewed against the judge output with discrepancies resolved by re-reading. To assess reliability, a second coder (a colleague without CS background and unaware of the hypotheses) independently coded a random 20-response subsample (7 A, 7 B, 6 C; masked, randomised). Agreement was $\kappa = 0.58$ (raw 85\%), moderate. All three discordant items were responses the first author coded as containing an analogy and the second coder did not, suggesting a systematic difference in the analogy boundary rather than random error; this is expected, since a coder without CS background may not recognise some technical comparisons as figurative. The second coder's labels were used only for reliability. We treat AP as an author-coded manipulation check rather than an independently validated annotation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\textbf{Ethics statement.} The two annotators were professional colleagues who participated as domain experts rather than as research participants, provided informed consent, and were compensated for their time; no personal or sensitive data were collected, and no institutional review board approval was required for this model-side audit under the applicable national regulations. All generated texts are stored without author identifiers, and the released corpus contains no information about the annotators beyond their anonymised role labels (h1, h2).

\subsection{Pilot Ablation: Simplification Without an Analogy Request}
\label{sec:method:pilot}

To obtain a first indication of whether analogies arise only because the simplified prompts request them, we added a pilot condition D after the main analysis. Condition D asks for an accurate, simplified explanation for a student with reading difficulties, with short sentences, and explicitly instructs the model not to use analogies (Appendix~\ref{appendix:prompts}). It was run once for each of the 35 concepts on a single model, mistral:7b, giving 35 responses. Because access to the HPC cluster was limited, the pilot was run on a local workstation via Ollama, with the same sampling parameters as the main corpus ($T = 0.7$, seed 42, 16,384-token context). No response appeared truncated on inspection.

The pilot is deliberately minimal: one model, one generation per concept, no LLM judge, and only analogy presence was coded (SM, DA, and OER were not rated). Analogy presence followed the definition given above. A preliminary keyword screen (e.g., \emph{like a}, \emph{imagine}, \emph{think of}) flagged 31 of 35 responses, but it over-detects (for instance, \emph{similar to} in purely technical comparisons), so presence was determined by reading each response. Coding was done by the first author, was not masked to condition (all responses came from D), and was not repeated by a second coder. Because a few responses contain brief comparisons whose status is ambiguous, we report a strict count and an inclusive count that adds these borderline cases. Proportions are given with Wilson 95\% intervals and are compared descriptively with the human-coded rates of A, B, and C from the validation subsample, which come from a different sample (30 responses per condition, all seven models); no formal test is applied.

\subsection{Readability Metrics}

As a manipulation check, readability was computed with \texttt{textstat}: Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) \cite{flesch1948new}, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), average sentence length (ASL), and word count:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{FRE} &= 206.835 - 1.015\,\frac{\text{words}}{\text{sentences}} - 84.6\,\frac{\text{syllables}}{\text{words}},\\
\mathrm{FKGL} &= 0.39\,\frac{\text{words}}{\text{sentences}} + 11.8\,\frac{\text{syllables}}{\text{words}} - 15.59.
\end{align}
These measure surface form, not comprehensibility, and technical terms can distort syllable counts; readability is therefore a manipulation check, not an outcome.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Plan}
\label{sec:method:stat}

Analyses used Python 3 (SciPy, statsmodels, textstat) and, for mixed models, R (lme4); scripts are in the repository. The threshold is $\alpha = 0.05$ with adjustments where indicated; proportions are reported with Wilson 95\% intervals.

\subsubsection{Omnibus Tests}

SM, DA, and accuracy were tested with one-way ANOVA across conditions (equivalent to a linear probability model for binary outcomes), complemented for binary outcomes by chi-square tests and Cochran's Q \cite{cochran1950comparison} with the concept-model pair as blocking unit. For readability metrics, Shapiro-Wilk \cite{shapiro1965analysis} and Levene tests \cite{levene1960robust} assessed normality and variance homogeneity.

\subsubsection{Paired and Clustered Analyses for Binary Outcomes}

Because the three conditions of a concept-model pair are not independent, we complemented ANOVA with exact pairwise McNemar tests \cite{mcnemar1947note} (Holm-corrected); a logistic GEE with exchangeable working correlation within pairs (odds ratios with Wald 95\% CIs); and for accuracy the Friedman test with Holm-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Spearman correlations with the binary indicators. Where ANOVA and these analyses disagree, we report both and interpret the more conservative.

\subsubsection{Mixed-Effects Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:method:glmm}

Concepts and models are crossed rather than nested, so a GLMM with random intercepts for both is in principle more appropriate than GEE, which does not separate concept and model variance. We fitted, for the primary judge on the full corpus,
\begin{equation}
  \text{logit}\,Y_{cmk} = \alpha + \beta_k + u_c + u_m, \qquad Y \in \{\mathrm{SM}, \mathrm{DA}, \mathrm{OER}\},
\end{equation}
where $\beta_k$ is the fixed effect of condition (reference A for SM and DA; B for OER, which is undefined in A), $u_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_c^2)$, and $u_m \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_m^2)$. Models were fitted with lme4 (1.1-35), \texttt{bobyqa}, logit link, Laplace approximation, with Wald 95\% CIs. The GLMM was not refitted for the secondary judge.

\subsubsection{Secondary Judge and Inter-Judge Agreement}

Omnibus, McNemar, and GEE analyses for SM, DA, and OER, and Friedman/Wilcoxon analyses for accuracy, were repeated for the secondary judge on all 735 responses. Inter-judge agreement on the 720 jointly scored responses is reported as Cohen's $\kappa$ and raw agreement (Boolean) and quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ and Spearman's $\rho$ (accuracy).

\subsubsection{Human-Consensus Analysis by Condition}

On the 30 validation groups we compute, for SM and DA, condition rates with Wilson intervals, Cochran's $Q$, and exact pairwise McNemar tests with Holm correction. For OER (B and C only) we report rates with Wilson intervals; given only 5 and 4 flags, no C vs.\ B test is attempted. This analysis was not in the original plan and is post hoc; with 30 groups per condition it has limited precision.

\subsubsection{Conditional DAR Analysis}

The decomposition in Equation~\ref{eq:dar_decomp} is estimated on the validation subsample: $p_{\text{analogy}}(k)$ is the proportion of responses in condition $k$ with AP $= 1$, and conditional DAR is the ratio of dangerous analogies to analogies, using consensus DA labels as primary estimate and the judges' labels for comparison. Because condition A contained no analogies, conditional DAR is undefined there (reported as n/a).

\subsubsection{Secondary Analyses}

A two-way ANOVA (Type II) with model and condition tests the model $\times$ condition interaction. One-way ANOVAs within each condition examine subfield differences, with false discovery rate controlled by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure \cite{benjamini1995controlling}. Each judge is validated against the consensus with Cohen's $\kappa$, precision, recall, and prevalence (quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ for accuracy). Position effects are examined by comparing rates across presentation positions, a position-adjusted GEE, and restricted analyses.

\subsubsection{Effect Sizes and Reproducibility}

Effect sizes (Cohen's $d$, $\eta^2$, Kendall's $W$, Cram\'{e}r's $V$) \cite{cohen1988statistical, lakens2013calculating} are interpreted alongside confidence intervals. The corpus, prompts, codebook, judge outputs, human annotations, analogy codes, and scripts are available in the repository (Data Availability); the Ollama version, model digests, and sampling parameters are logged with the corpus.

\subsection{Strategic Misconception Rate (SMR)}

Table~\ref{tab:sm} reports SMR for the primary judge: 30 of 240 in A ($12.5\%$, 95\% CI $[8.9, 17.3]$), 41 in B ($17.1\%$, $[12.8, 22.4]$), and 33 in C ($13.8\%$, $[10.0, 18.7]$). The differences are not significant: ANOVA $F(2, 717) = 1.09$, $p = 0.337$, $\eta^2 = 0.003$; chi-square $\chi^2(2) = 2.18$, $p = 0.336$; Cochran's $Q(2) = 2.37$, $p = 0.306$; Holm-corrected McNemar contrasts all non-significant. The GEE gives OR $= 1.44$ (95\% CI $[0.88, 2.36]$, $p = 0.146$) for B vs.\ A and 1.12 ($[0.66, 1.88]$, $p = 0.680$) for C vs.\ A.

On the primary-judge data H1 is therefore not supported. This null result must be read with Section~\ref{sec:judgeval}: the primary judge flagged $12.5\%$ of standard responses as strategic misconceptions where the human consensus flagged none of 30, and the consensus shows a monotonic increase ($0\%$, $16.7\%$, $26.7\%$) that the primary judge does not reproduce. Model identity predicts the primary judge's SMR much more strongly than condition (Table~\ref{tab:models}, Panel B; model $F(6, 699) = 5.76$, $p < 0.001$).

\textbf{Mixed-effects sensitivity analysis.} The GLMM gives OR $= 1.50$ (95\% CI $[0.89, 2.52]$, $p = 0.129$) for B vs.\ A and 1.13 ($[0.66, 1.94]$, $p = 0.663$) for C vs.\ A, close to the GEE estimates and non-significant. Random-effect SDs are $\sigma_c = 0.65$ (concept) and $\sigma_m = 0.95$ (model): between-model variation dominates (Figure~\ref{fig:smr_by_model}).

\textbf{Secondary judge.} It flagged fewer SMs overall but a different condition pattern (Table~\ref{tab:secondary}): 6 of 245 in A ($2.4\%$, $[1.1, 5.2]$), 15 in B ($6.1\%$, $[3.7, 9.9]$), and 16 in C ($6.5\%$, $[4.1, 10.3]$). \subsection{Dangerous Analogy Rate (DAR)}

Table~\ref{tab:da} shows that, for the primary judge, A produced 2 dangerous analogies in 240 responses ($0.8\%$, 95\% CI $[0.2, 3.0]$), B produced 49 ($20.4\%$, $[15.8, 26.0]$), and C produced 59 ($24.6\%$, $[19.6, 30.4]$). Omnibus tests confirm a large effect: ANOVA $F(2, 717) = 32.38$, $p < 0.001$, $\eta^2 = 0.083$; chi-square $\chi^2(2) = 59.64$, $p < 0.001$, $V = 0.288$; Cochran's $Q(2) = 58.51$, $p < 0.001$. B vs.\ A ($b = 48$, $c = 1$) and C vs.\ A ($b = 58$, $c = 1$) are significant (Holm $p < 0.001$); C vs.\ B is not ($b = 46$, $c = 36$, $p = 0.320$). GEE odds ratios are $30.53$ (95\% CI $[7.48, 124.54]$) for B vs.\ A and $38.79$ ($[9.50, 158.37]$) for C vs.\ A. The very wide intervals reflect the near-zero baseline.

\textbf{Mixed-effects sensitivity analysis.} The GLMM gives larger odds ratios: $36.03$ ($[8.81, 147.36]$) for B vs.\ A and $47.34$ ($[11.60, 193.14]$) for C vs.\ A (both $p < 0.001$), with $\sigma_c = 0.58$ and $\sigma_m = 0.81$. The effect is robust to the repeated-measures specification; the GEE, if anything, understates it.

\textbf{Secondary judge.} It reproduces the effect and also separates B and C (Table~\ref{tab:secondary}): 4 of 245 in A ($1.6\%$, $[0.6, 4.1]$), 76 in B ($31.0\%$, $[25.6, 37.1]$), and 135 in C ($55.1\%$, $[48.8, 61.2]$). GEE odds ratios are 27.09 ($[9.68, 75.81]$) for B vs.\ A, 73.94 ($[26.59, 205.65]$) for C vs.\ A, and 2.73 ($[1.95, 3.83]$) for C vs.\ B (McNemar $b = 84$, $c = 25$, Holm $p < 0.001$).

First, all 30 standard responses were technical descriptions without an external referent. Second, every simplified response (30 of 30 in B and in C) contained an analogy, as the prompts request. Third, because frequency is saturated, conditional DAR equals unconditional DAR on this subsample: $30.0\%$ in B and $36.7\%$ in C by consensus, a non-significant difference (Section~\ref{sec:judgeval}).

Conditional rates depend on the rater: the primary judge flags $13.3\%$ and $16.7\%$, the secondary judge $23.3\%$ and $43.3\%$, and the annotators' raw ratings span a wider range (Table~\ref{tab:reviewer:rates}). All raters agree on the ordering: zero in A, a substantial rate in B, and a numerically higher rate in C.

Because the baseline contains no analogies, the increase of unconditional DAR from A to B and C is mechanically produced by analogy frequency ($0\%$ to $100\%$); no change in analogy quality is needed to explain it, and the quality of baseline analogies cannot be observed. But exposure is not harmless: conditional on an analogy being present, roughly three in ten (B) to almost four in ten (C) were dangerous by consensus. The supported form of the trade-off is therefore: accessibility-style prompts reliably convert an explanation into an analogy-based one, and a substantial fraction of the analogies produced mislead about a key property. Frequency creates the exposure, per-analogy danger determines the harm of each exposure, and the present design can quantify the second only for B and C.

\subsection{Pilot Ablation: Analogies Under an Explicit Prohibition}
\label{sec:pilot}

Of the 35 responses to the pilot prompt D (mistral:7b), 20 contained a clear analogy (57.1\%, 95\% CI $[40.8, 72.0]$), and 23 (65.7\%, $[49.7, 78.7]$) when three borderline cases are included; 12 contained none (Table~\ref{tab:pilot}). The explicit prohibition therefore did not remove analogies. Typical examples were a hash table as a box of toys, garbage collection as a clean-up crew, threads as factory workers, a CPU scheduler as a traffic cop, and semaphores as traffic lights, which is the pattern named as a dangerous example in our codebook. Responses without an analogy were mostly short technical descriptions. Danger was not rated in the pilot.

For reference, the human-coded validation subsample contained no analogy in the standard condition (0 of 30) and one in every simplified response (30 of 30 in B and in C). The pilot rate is numerically between these values, but the comparison is descriptive only, because the reference rates come from a different sample (all seven models) and the pilot has no same-model standard baseline. The pilot suggests that, for this model, the audience-and-simplification framing pushes the response towards analogies even without an analogy request, so that the main DAR increase is unlikely to be purely a consequence of instruction-following. The evidence is weak: one small model that may follow negative instructions poorly, one generation per concept, an unmasked single coder, and a prompt that retains the audience label, so the contribution of the persona and of simplification itself cannot be separated.

\subsection{Oversimplification Error Rate (OER)}

By construction OER cannot occur in A; the single response flagged there by the primary judge is treated as noise. For the primary judge (Table~\ref{tab:oer}), OER was 16 of 240 in B ($6.7\%$, 95\% CI $[4.1, 10.6]$) and 10 in C ($4.2\%$, $[2.3, 7.5]$). Both intervals exclude zero, but the expected increase from B to C does not appear.

We also derived OER from the SM indicators: the proportion of simplified responses with SM $= 1$ whose standard response for the same pair has SM $= 0$. This gives 34 of 240 in B ($14.2\%$, $[10.3, 19.1]$) and 28 in C ($11.7\%$, $[8.2, 16.3]$). This must be read with care: because generation was stochastic ($T = 0.7$) and the judge is imperfect, the opposite transition (SM $= 1$ in A, $0$ in the simplified response) also occurs, in 23 pairs for B and 25 for C (Table~\ref{tab:sm}, Panel C, column $c$). The net excess of simplified-only misconceptions is only 11 pairs for B and 3 for C, so the derived rates partly reflect sampling noise.

The human consensus, rated directly against the standard response, flagged 5 of 30 in B ($16.7\%$, $[7.3, 33.6]$) and 4 of 30 in C ($13.3\%$, $[5.3, 29.7]$), close to the derived measure and two to three times the primary judge's rate. The secondary judge flagged an OER in $77.6\%$ of B and $84.1\%$ of C responses, and in $7.3\%$ of standard responses where the indicator is undefined (Table~\ref{tab:secondary}). These rates are about five times the consensus and show the criterion of an error absent from the baseline was not applied; the secondary judge's OER output is not interpretable as an error rate, and its significant C vs.\ B contrast is not taken as evidence for H4.

\textbf{Mixed-effects sensitivity analysis.} The GLMM for OER (B and C, B as reference) gives OR $= 0.60$ for C vs.\ B ($[0.27, 1.37]$, $p = 0.225$), with $\sigma_c = 0.62$ and $\sigma_m = 0.32$: OER varies more by concept than by model. This is consistent with the exploratory concept-level pattern in Appendix~\ref{appendix:concepts}.

OER by model ranges from $2.9\%$ (gemma4:31b, gpt-oss:20b, ministral-3:14b) to $10.3\%$ (mistral:7b), with no clear relation to size: phi4:14b ($10.0\%$) is close to mistral:7b.

\subsection{Model Comparison}
\label{sec:models}

Table~\ref{tab:models} reports primary-judge error rates and accuracy by model, pooled over conditions. \subsection{Position Effects in the Judge}

Because the primary judge saw all three conditions jointly in random order, we examined order effects. Its SMR rose from $9.6\%$ at position 1 to $21.2\%$ at position 3; DAR ($10.8\%$, $15.8\%$, $19.2\%$) and OER ($0.6\%$, $4.7\%$, $11.2\%$) also rose, and accuracy fell (4.50, 4.38, 4.13). Position was only mildly unbalanced across conditions (A first 70 times, B 84, C 86), so the effect adds noise rather than systematically favouring one condition. For OER the dependence is especially visible: simplified responses judged after the baseline had $10.0\%$ OER against $1.2\%$ when the baseline came later.

After adjusting the GEE for position, the DAR effect remains large (B vs.\ A OR $= 30.97$ $[7.65, 125.48]$; C vs.\ A OR $= 39.81$ $[9.82, 161.37]$; both $p < 0.001$) and SMR contrasts remain non-significant (OR $= 1.47$ $[0.89, 2.42]$, $p = 0.134$; OR $= 1.13$ $[0.66, 1.94]$, $p = 0.643$). Restricting to responses shown first, SMR was not higher in simplified conditions ($15.7\%$, $7.1\%$, $7.0\%$) while DAR remained elevated ($1.4\%$, $13.1\%$, $16.3\%$). The DAR finding is thus robust to the order effect, whereas SMR and especially OER judgments are order-dependent and less reliable. The secondary judge's DAR also rose with position ($11.8\%$, $34.7\%$, $41.2\%$); its analyses were not position-adjusted.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

The summary below integrates the results across raters and robustness checks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (SMR).} Rater-dependent. The primary judge detects no significant increase ($12.5\%, 17.1\%, 13.8\%$; GLMM OR $= 1.50$, $1.13$), but it flags $12.5\%$ of standard responses that the consensus flags at $0\%$ and has $8\%$ recall. The consensus shows a monotonic increase ($0\%, 16.7\%, 26.7\%$; $Q$ $p = 0.012$; C vs.\ A Holm $p = 0.023$), and the secondary judge a smaller borderline one ($2.4\%, 6.1\%, 6.5\%$). H1 is supported by the human consensus, weakly by the secondary judge, and not by the primary judge.
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (DAR).} Supported under every rater for A $<$ B and A $<$ C: $0.8\%$ to $20.4\%$ and $24.6\%$ (primary; GEE ORs about 31 and 39, GLMM 36 and 47), $1.6\%$ to $31.0\%$ and $55.1\%$ (secondary), $0\%$ to $30.0\%$ and $36.7\%$ (consensus). C $\geq$ B holds numerically for all raters but is significant only for the secondary judge.
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (frequency vs.\ quality).} In the validation subsample the standard condition contained no analogies and every simplified response one, so the DAR increase is produced by analogy frequency (H3a supported) and the baseline per-analogy rate cannot be observed. Conditional DAR is substantial, $30.0\%$ in B and $36.7\%$ in C by consensus (H3b supported in the sense of being non-negligible; it cannot be compared with A and does not differ significantly between B and C).
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (OER).} Measurable ($6.7\%, 4.2\%$ primary; $16.7\%, 13.3\%$ consensus; $14.2\%, 11.7\%$ derived) but not increasing from B to C (GLMM OR $= 0.60$). The secondary judge's OER output is not interpretable. H4 is partially supported: existence yes, monotonicity no.
    \item \textbf{RQ5 (moderators).} Model is a strong source of variation; the secondary judge reproduces the ranking. Subfield effects do not survive correction; the model $\times$ condition interaction is not significant.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The discussion interprets the results against the accessibility trade-off hypothesis, explains why the rater-dependence is itself a methodological finding, and outlines a mechanistic reading of why vulnerable learners may be at risk. It also addresses oversimplification errors and implications for evaluation and mitigation. Throughout, we separate what the data show from what remains hypothesis.

\subsection{Testing the Accessibility Trade-off}

The verdict is nuanced, largely because it depends on who does the rating. The strongest and most consistent finding is that dangerous analogies are more frequent under accessibility-style prompts, under all three raters and all robustness checks. Strategic misconceptions rise by human consensus and weakly by the secondary judge but not by the primary judge, so H1 is supported by the best available standard but not by the cheapest instrument. Oversimplification errors are measurable but do not grow with intensity (H4 partially supported). The frequency/quality decomposition shows that the DAR increase is mechanically produced by the appearance of analogies, and that roughly three in ten are dangerous by the human standard.

It is important what this does and does not show. Because B and C explicitly request analogies and A does not, we cannot say that models spontaneously reach for analogies when simplifying, nor separate the effect of simplification from that of the analogy request, the missing accuracy cue, or the audience label (Section~\ref{sec:method}). What the data show is that the bundle of constraints typical of accessibility-oriented requests reliably yields analogy-based explanations, that a substantial fraction mislead about a key property, and that explanations also more often contain a plausible but wrong claim by the human standard. The pilot ablation (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}) gives a first, limited indication on this point: with an explicit prohibition of analogies, mistral:7b still produced one in 20 of 35 responses (57.1\%), a rate numerically below the 100\% observed under B and C but far above the 0\% observed under A. This suggests that the audience-and-simplification framing itself, and not only the analogy request, pushes this model towards analogies. The evidence is weak (one small model, one generation per concept, no same-model baseline, an unmasked single coder), and separating the contribution of each prompt element still requires a full ablation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The pattern is consistent with the analogy literature: good analogies transfer relational structure, not surface attributes \cite{gentner1983structure}, whereas the analogies observed tend to transfer surface attributes (a semaphore signals go/stop) and drop relational structure (atomicity, counting). \citet{stone2024analogies} documented this for GPT-4 under standard prompting; our results indicate that accessibility-style prompts make it routine.

That C does not exceed B significantly (primary judge and consensus) admits several readings: the five-sentence limit may leave little room to develop an analogy into a misconception; the primary judge's low recall makes its estimates noisy; or the human sample (30 groups per condition) cannot resolve a seven-point difference. The secondary judge separates B and C for DAR ($31.0\%$ vs.\ $55.1\%$), the only evidence for a dose-response relationship, but it agrees with the consensus on which analogies are dangerous only at $\kappa = 0.16$. A B--C gradient is therefore plausible but unresolved.

\subsection{The Methodological Contribution: The Limits of LLM-as-Judge}

The primary judge agreed with the consensus at $\kappa = 0.03$ (SM) and $0.16$ (DA), with recall of $8\%$ and $20\%$; the secondary judge matched DA prevalence but with precision and recall of 0.35 and produced unusable OER output; the annotators themselves initially disagreed on DA ($\kappa = 0.11$), showing the construct is subjective and needs adjudication. The choice of judge changed the conclusion for H1. Evaluations that rely on a single LLM judge without a human-labelled calibration set can both miss effects (primary judge, SMR) and fabricate them (secondary judge, OER). We recommend hybrid designs in which the LLM is a scalable pre-screener and experts validate a stratified subsample, with transparency about the limitations of both. Note that the two judges are from the same family; a multi-family ensemble might behave differently.

\subsection{Why Vulnerable Learners May Be at Risk}

The following is a theoretical framework for reading the results; our experiment does not measure learner cognition. First, asymmetry of verification: a simplified explanation removes cues a reader would use to notice omissions, and novices lack schemas to flag gaps \cite{kalyuga2003expertise}. Second, load: if decoding or attention regulation absorbs working-memory resources \cite{baddeley1974working, laberge1974toward, sweller1998cognitive}, checking claims against prior knowledge competes with comprehension. Third, fluent statements gain a truth premium \cite{reber1999effects, alter2009uniting} and short self-contained accounts produce untested feelings of understanding \cite{rozenblit2002misunderstood}. Fourth, a vivid analogy is memorable by design, so a flawed one is memorably flawed, and a single analogy without a stated limit is the textbook recipe for analogy-induced misconception \cite{spiro1989multiple, glynn1991explaining}. Finally, private deployment removes external checks.

The vulnerability is situational, produced jointly by learner load and material properties, and the same reasoning predicts risk for any novice under time pressure or fatigue. It is not a claim that learners with dyslexia or ADHD are less capable of critical thought, nor that simplification should be abandoned; only a study with learners can establish how strongly each factor contributes.

\subsection{Oversimplification Errors}

The primary-judge OER rates ($6.7\%$, $4.2\%$), consensus rates ($16.7\%$, $13.3\%$), and derived rates ($14.2\%$, $11.7\%$) indicate that a measurable share of simplified responses contain an error absent from the same model's standard response, though part of the derived measure reflects sampling noise. The GLMM result (OR $= 0.60$, non-significant) and near-equal consensus rates indicate that OER does not grow with intensity, with the caveat that only 5 and 4 consensus flags are available. The largest SMR increases (primary judge) occurred for semaphores ($+35.7$ pp), normalization and the CAP theorem ($+28.6$ each), dynamic programming vs.\ greedy and transaction isolation ($+25.0$ each), and ACID and the TLS handshake ($+21.4$ each): concepts defined by necessary conditions, invariants, or boundary cases, the properties analogy-based simplification tends to drop. This is post hoc, rests on at most seven pairs per concept, and relies on the primary judge's low-sensitivity SM indicator. It is, however, consistent with the OER random-effects decomposition ($\sigma_c = 0.62 > \sigma_m = 0.32$).

We distinguish five illustrative OER subtypes that were not coded systematically: (i) omission of a necessary condition; (ii) promotion of an analogy from illustration to definition; (iii) over-generalisation of a special case; (iv) conflation with a neighbouring concept; and (v) replacement of a mechanism by an outcome (Appendix~\ref{appendix:analogies}). Because CS knowledge is hierarchical \cite{benari2001constructivism} and wrong models resist later instruction \cite{chi1994things, posner1982accommodation}, errors at foundational concepts such as semaphores, normalization, or the CAP theorem may be especially costly.

\subsection{Ablation Plan and Implications for Evaluation and Mitigation}

First, accuracy on a neutral prompt is an insufficient safety criterion for an educational assistant; models should be evaluated under the audience and instruction they will actually receive \cite{lin2022truthfulqa}. Second, standard quality metrics (readability, fluency) can mask semantic degradation \cite{devaraj2022evaluating, trienes2024infolossqa}: B is the most readable condition yet shows a markedly higher DAR under every rater. Third, the safety-relevant quantity is both per-response error rate and analogy density. Fourth, single-judge evaluation without human calibration is unreliable.

Mitigation can act at the prompt, model, or interface level. At the prompt level, instructions can require boundary conditions and an explicit statement of where each analogy fails \cite{glynn1991explaining}, multiple complementary analogies \cite{spiro1989multiple}, or alternation of analogies with technical statements; generate-then-verify pipelines can compare a simplified explanation with a ground-truth checklist. At the model level, between-model variation was large in this corpus, so model choice may matter, but since family and scale are confounded and no intervention was tested, we do not claim that switching models is an effective remedy. None of these mitigations was evaluated here; they are hypotheses that follow from the observed pattern.

\section{Interface Design Hypotheses}
\label{sec:design}

This section is conceptual and separate from the empirical results: the proposals below were not tested with learners, and we present them as hypotheses motivated by the findings and by established work on accessible design. They draw on Universal Design for Learning and ability-based design \cite{rose2002teaching, wobbrock2011ability}, on pre-attentive visual features \cite{treisman1980feature}, and on uncertainty communication research \cite{hullman2019pursuit}. Their aim is to avoid relying solely on text-based disclaimers, which are additional text to decode for learners with reading difficulties.

\textbf{Hypothesis 1: analogy status tagging.} The most directly motivated proposal follows from the finding that accessibility-style prompts yield an analogy in essentially every response and that about one in three is dangerous by the human standard. Interfaces could tag analogies explicitly (e.g., a badge reading ``Analogy''), offer a toggle to a technical version, highlight the analogy's span, and provide on demand a one-line statement of the main property the analogy does not share with the target \cite{glynn1991explaining}, optionally with a second, complementary analogy \cite{spiro1989multiple}. This operationalises the teaching-with-analogies recommendation to state where an analogy breaks. Span marking could be obtained through structured model output; the reliability of such self-tagging is an empirical question.

\textbf{Hypothesis 2: visual uncertainty signalling.} Low-confidence responses could carry a persistent, standardised icon with progressive disclosure of a plain-language explanation, processed pre-attentively \cite{treisman1980feature}. Colour must not be the sole channel; every cue needs a programmatic text alternative for screen-reader and text-to-speech users \cite{w3c2023wcag}. A signal is only as trustworthy as its estimator: verbalised confidence is poorly calibrated, and our judge validation shows the risk. An evaluator with low recall would flag few of the responses an expert would flag, creating false safety, and one with high recall but low precision (the secondary judge on OER) would flag nearly everything and teach users to ignore the signal.

\textbf{Hypothesis 3: low-load, customisable presentation.} Signals should be few, consistent, and learnable, since added indicators add load \cite{sweller1998cognitive}; passive, glanceable cues are preferable to cognitive forcing functions that reduce over-reliance but increase effort and are less liked \cite{bucinca2021trust}. Animation, if used, should be optional and off under reduced-motion preferences, and users should be able to select themes (e.g., high contrast) without losing signalling \cite{rose2002teaching, wobbrock2011ability}.

Evaluating these hypotheses requires a study with learners with and without dyslexia and ADHD, comparing interfaces with and without the signals on acceptance of a deliberately flawed explanation, later unassisted performance (following \cite{bastani2025generative}), perceived workload, and usability. Such a study would complement, not replace, the model-side audit presented here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations are organised by their likely impact on the main claims. The first three are the most consequential: the absence of learner data, the bundled prompt design, and the reliability of the LLM judges. The remaining limitations concern the corpus, the human reference standard, and the statistical specification.

\subsection{No Learner Data}

Evaluation was by two LLM judges and two experts on a subsample, not by students. We do not measure whether strategic misconceptions produce learning deficits, nor whether plausibility predicts belief change. The dyslexia and ADHD conditions are prompt personas that cannot represent the heterogeneity of real learners, and the prompts encode the assumption that these learners benefit from simplification and analogy, itself a design choice rather than an established requirement.

\subsection{Domain, Language, and Models}

The benchmark covers CS and English only. The seven models (none above 32B parameters, quantised via Ollama) represent a fraction of available LLMs, and the pattern may differ for proprietary frontier models or full-precision deployments. Open weights were chosen to ensure full reproducibility.

\subsection{Prompt Design and Construct Validity}

Conditions are bundles of constraints; the accuracy cue appears only in A; B discourages formal definitions; B and C request analogies. Results therefore describe behaviour under realistic accessibility-style requests, not the causal effect of any single element. The central DAR increase is largely a consequence of the analogy request and should be interpreted accordingly. A pilot condition D (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}) partly addresses the analogy request, but only for one model, with one generation per concept, without a same-model standard baseline, without a variant that omits the audience label, and without ratings of misconceptions. The full ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation:plan} remains the most important extension. Each cell also contains a single generation, so within-cell sampling variability is not estimated.

\subsection{Frequency Versus Quality}

Analogy frequency saturates in the validation subsample, so conditional DAR is estimable only for B and C, with wide intervals ($[16.7, 47.9]$ and $[21.9, 54.5]$). We cannot say whether per-analogy danger in simplified responses exceeds that in standard explanations. Analogy presence was coded by one author with the label masked (second-coder $\kappa = 0.58$ on 20 responses, with unidirectional disagreements), so conditional rates inherit that operationalisation; we treat them as exploratory. A full-corpus analysis with several independent coders and a baseline that also requests an analogy would separate the channels fully.

\subsection{Causal Interpretation of OER}

Attributing an error present in a simplified but absent in the standard response to simplification ignores random variation at $T = 0.7$; the frequency of the opposite transition shows this is not minor. Replication at $T = 0.0$ or with several samples per cell would help but reduce ecological validity.

\subsection{LLM-Judge Validity}

Neither judge substitutes for the human standard (Table~\ref{tab:judge:validation}). The primary judge's absolute rates are probably underestimates for simplified conditions and possibly overestimates for SM in A. The judges share a model family and are not independent. Joint presentation of the three conditions introduces order effects (primary judge flags roughly twice as many errors at position 3 as at position 1); position adjustment does not change DAR or SMR contrasts, but SMR and OER comparisons remain order-dependent, and the secondary judge was not position-adjusted.

\subsection{Reliability of the Human Standard}

Pre-adjudication agreement was slight to fair for Boolean indicators ($\kappa = 0.283$, $0.110$, $0.317$) and annotators applied clearly different DA thresholds (h1 $56.7\%$ and $76.7\%$, h2 $6.7\%$ in B and C). Consensus labels come from a meeting of the same two annotators, without recorded justifications and without blinding to each other's ratings, so discussion dynamics may have influenced them. Both annotators agree on direction, so our conclusions rest on direction and ordering rather than exact magnitudes; in particular the consensus DA rates of $30.0\%$ and $36.7\%$ depend on the adjudication outcome. The consensus condition analysis was post hoc, based on 30 groups per condition, and borderline for SM (C vs.\ A Holm $p = 0.023$). Annotators could plausibly infer condition from response form. The accuracy comparison uses h1 as reference (two-annotator mean 3.67 vs.\ 3.39).

\subsection{Parsing Failures and Statistical Specification}

Fifteen responses from five groups (four involving DeepSeek-R1 generators) were lost for the primary judge; we have not established whether the cause lies in the response text or the judge. The secondary judge parsed all groups. The GEE does not separate concept and model variance; the GLMM does and agrees qualitatively (GLMM DAR estimates are larger, so GEE if anything understates the effect). The GLMM uses the Laplace approximation and normal random intercepts and was not fitted for the secondary judge.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced AccessTrap-CS, a corpus of 735 responses from seven open-weight LLMs to 35 CS concepts under standard, simplified, and highly simplified prompts, with an error taxonomy (strategic misconceptions, dangerous analogies, oversimplification errors), two LLM judges, and validation against a 90-response expert-annotated subsample with adjudicated consensus labels. The main empirical claims rest on the human consensus, with the judges treated as scalable indicators whose calibration is itself a result.

Accessibility-style prompts markedly increased dangerous analogies under every rater (human consensus $0\%$ to $30.0\%$ and $36.7\%$; primary judge $0.8\%$ to $20.4\%$ and $24.6\%$; secondary judge $1.6\%$ to $31.0\%$ and $55.1\%$), and the effect survived order adjustment, restriction to first-shown responses, and a crossed random-effects model. In the validation subsample every simplified response contained an analogy, as requested, and $30$--$37\%$ of these were dangerous by consensus. By human consensus strategic misconceptions also rose ($0.0\%$, $16.7\%$, $26.7\%$; C vs.\ A significant after correction), whereas the primary judge detected no increase, which we attribute to its low recall and false positives in the standard condition. Oversimplification errors exist but do not grow with intensity, and model identity is as large a source of variation as prompt condition.

These findings come with essential caveats. Because the prompts bundle constraints and explicitly request analogies, the study demonstrates the risk of requesting analogy-based simplification, not a causal effect of simplification itself. The human reference is based on 30 groups per condition and two annotators with only slight to fair pre-adjudication agreement. Both LLM judges agreed poorly with the consensus ($\kappa \leq 0.16$ for SM and DA), and the choice of judge changed the conclusion for H1. Of all results, the DAR increase withstands every check we ran. A small single-model pilot suggests that analogies are not solely a matter of instruction-following: a simplified prompt that forbade analogies still elicited them in 20 of 35 responses (57.1\%), although this needs confirmation across models.

Practically, analogy tagging with stated limits and hybrid human--LLM evaluation are the most immediately actionable implications; the interface proposals in Section~\ref{sec:design} remain hypotheses. Future work should add the ablation conditions of Table~\ref{tab:ablation:plan}, multiple samples per cell, and independent coders for analogy presence on the full corpus; extend to other domains and languages; improve judge calibration through multi-family ensembles; and test the proposed interface ideas with learners using learning-outcome measures.

\section[\appendixname~\thesection]{Prompt Templates}
\label{appendix:prompts}

The three templates are reproduced verbatim; \texttt{[CONCEPT]} is replaced by the concept name.

\noindent\textbf{Condition A (standard).}
\begin{verbatim}
Explain the following computer science concept accurately and clearly, 
suitable for a first-year computer science student.

Answer in English.

Concept: [CONCEPT]
\end{verbatim}

\noindent\textbf{Condition B (simplified, reading difficulties).}
\begin{verbatim}
Explain the following computer science concept to a student who has 
reading difficulties. Use simplified language, short sentences, and 
concrete analogies instead of code or formal definitions. Avoid 
technical jargon where possible.

Answer in English.

Concept: [CONCEPT]
\end{verbatim}

\noindent\textbf{Condition C (highly simplified, attention difficulties).}
\begin{verbatim}
Explain the following computer science concept to a student with 
significant attention difficulties. Keep the explanation within five 
sentences. Use one single, vivid analogy that captures the core idea. 
Make it memorable and engaging.

Answer in English.

Concept: [CONCEPT]
\end{verbatim}

\noindent\textbf{Condition D (pilot ablation; simplified, analogies forbidden).}
\begin{verbatim}
Explain the following computer science concept accurately to a 
student who has reading difficulties. Use simplified language and 
short sentences. Do not use analogies.

Answer in English.

Concept: [CONCEPT]
\end{verbatim}

Templates A--C differ only in the instruction line; D is used only in the pilot ablation (Section~\ref{sec:pilot}). They are deliberately short, mirroring how a student or teacher would phrase a request to a general-purpose assistant; this increases ecological validity at the cost of construct specificity (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section[\appendixname~\thesection]{Evaluation Codebook}
\label{appendix:codebook}

The codebook below was given to the two human annotators (CS educators) and mirrors the definitions used for the LLM judges.

\textbf{Annotation protocol.} Annotators rated 90 responses (30 concept-model groups, stratified by subfield, models rotated for balance) independently. In Part 1 the 90 responses were presented in a single random order, one at a time, with the reference definition and without any indication of generating model, condition, or judge output; each was rated for SM, DA, and accuracy. Because OER is a contrast with the standard response, it was rated in Part 2, after Part 1, on 30 blocks each consisting of the standard response and the two simplified responses (B and C, random order) of the same group, giving 60 ratings. Disagreements on SM, DA, and OER were resolved by discussion to a consensus label; accuracy scores were not adjudicated.

\textbf{General rules.} Judge technical content only. Ignore style, tone, length, formatting, friendliness, and emojis. The reference definition is your anchor but only one formulation: a correct statement that differs from its wording is not an error; a statement that contradicts the reference or established CS knowledge is. Do not penalise omissions as such; an omission counts only if it makes the explanation misleading, i.e., leads to a false belief. Rate every response on its own; do not compare responses to the same concept in Part 1. Do not use AI tools; you may consult textbooks. Work independently and do not discuss items until the adjudication meeting.

\textbf{Strategic misconception (SM): 0 or 1.} SM = 1 if the response contains at least one conceptually significant error that is stated plausibly and coherently, so that a student would not notice it and would build a wrong mental model (what the concept is, how it works, when it applies, or how it differs from a neighbouring concept). NOT an SM: typos, a wrong date or name, harmless imprecision, loose wording that is not false, incompleteness that does not mislead. SM = 1 example: ``A deadlock is when processes simply wait for a long time.'' SM = 1 example: ``Segmentation eliminates external fragmentation.'' SM = 0 example: a wrong publication year. If unsure whether an error is plausible and consequential, choose 1 and add a short comment.

\textbf{Dangerous analogy (DA): 0 or 1.} DA = 1 if the response uses an analogy, metaphor, or everyday comparison that is intuitive but misrepresents a key technical property, i.e., invites a false inference about the concept. Ask: what would a student infer from this analogy that is false for the concept? If that inference concerns a key property, DA = 1. An analogy is not dangerous merely because it is incomplete; if the response states where it breaks down, or the analogy only leaves out detail without inviting a false inference, DA = 0. No analogy: DA = 0. DA = 1 example: a semaphore as a traffic light. DA and SM are independent.

\textbf{Accuracy score: 1 to 5.} 5 = fully accurate. 4 = accurate overall; minor imprecision or a small omission that does not mislead. 3 = partly accurate; a notable omission or inaccuracy that could mislead. 2 = major error(s); an important part of the core concept is wrong. 1 = fundamentally wrong, or does not address the concept.

\textbf{Part 2: Oversimplification error (OER): 0 or 1.} For each block you see the reference, one STANDARD response (prompt asking for an accurate explanation for a first-year student) and two SIMPLIFIED responses, labelled a and b (prompts asking for simplification for a student with reading or attention difficulties). Rate each simplified response separately; the standard response is only the reference point. OER = 1 if the simplified response contains an error or misleading statement NOT present in the standard response, i.e., one that exists only because of the simplification. Typical forms: a necessary condition dropped so the claim becomes false; an analogy promoted to a definition; a special case over-generalised; confusion with a neighbouring concept; a mechanism replaced by an outcome. OER = 0 if the simplified response is merely shorter or less detailed but contains no false or misleading statement, or if the same error is already present in the standard response.

\textbf{Analogy presence (AP).} Not rated by the annotators; coded separately by the first author (with a second coder on a 20-response reliability subsample). AP = 1 if the response contains at least one analogy, metaphor, or simile: an explicit comparison (like, as, think of, imagine), a metaphor, or an everyday comparison to a non-technical object or situation. Formal definitions, purely technical comparisons, and illustrative examples without an external referent do not count.

\section[\appendixname~\thesection]{LLM Judge Prompt}
\label{appendix:judgeprompt}

Both judges received the prompt below. It presents the concept, subfield, reference definition, and the three responses of one concept-model group in randomised order, each labelled with its target audience, and asks for a JSON object with one entry per response. Output is constrained by a JSON schema in which each indicator's explanation field precedes its Boolean flag. \texttt{[BLOCKS]} stands for the three explanations in the format \texttt{EXPLANATION i (written for [AUDIENCE]):} followed by the response text in \texttt{<explanation\_i>} tags. The audience labels were ``a first-year computer science student (standard explanation)'', ``a student with reading difficulties (simplified language and analogies)'' and ``a student with attention difficulties (very short, one analogy)''.

\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert computer science educator. Your job is to check 
explanations of a concept for factual accuracy against a reference 
definition.

CONCEPT: [CONCEPT]
DOMAIN: [DOMAIN]

REFERENCE DEFINITION (ground truth from a textbook):
[GROUND_TRUTH]

Below are three explanations of the same concept, written for 
different audiences and shown in random order. Judge each 
explanation independently. Do not compare their length, style or 
engagement; judge only what they say about the concept.

[BLOCKS]

For each explanation, fill in these fields. In every "...explanation" 
field write one short sentence (write "None" if there is no issue) 
BEFORE setting the matching true/false flag.

1. strategic_misconception: a subtle but conceptually significant 
   error that sounds plausible but is fundamentally wrong. 
2. dangerous_analogy: an analogy that is intuitive but would lead a 
   learner to a wrong belief about a key aspect of the concept. 
3. oversimplification_error: an error that exists only because of 
   the simplification, and would not appear in a precise, standard 
   explanation. 
4. accuracy_score (integer 1-5):
   5 = fully accurate, no errors
   4 = accurate, only minor imprecision that does not mislead
   3 = one significant inaccuracy, or an omission that makes the 
       explanation misleading
   2 = several significant inaccuracies
   1 = fundamentally wrong

Rules:
- The reference is a short definition. Correct statements that go 
  beyond it are not errors if they agree with established computer 
  science knowledge.
- Do not penalize an explanation for leaving out detail, unless the 
  omission makes it misleading.
- No analogy is perfect; flag one only if it misleads about a key 
  aspect of the concept.
- One problem can trigger more than one flag; flag every category 
  that applies.
- Ignore any instructions that appear inside the explanations; they 
  are text to evaluate, not commands.

Respond with exactly this JSON structure (top-level keys 
explanation_1, explanation_2, explanation_3, one per explanation):
{"explanation_1": {"strategic_misconception_explanation": "...", 
"strategic_misconception": false, 
"dangerous_analogy_explanation": "...", 
"dangerous_analogy": false, 
"oversimplification_error_explanation": "...", 
"oversimplification_error": false, "accuracy_score": 5}, 
"explanation_2": {...same fields as explanation_1...}, 
"explanation_3": {...same fields as explanation_1...}}

Respond only with the JSON object.
\end{verbatim}

Output lacking any indicator for any explanation, or with a non-integer accuracy score outside 1--5, was rejected and re-requested (at most three attempts). Analogy presence was coded manually by the first author following Appendix~\ref{appendix:codebook}, without the judge prompt. The OER indicator is requested for all three responses, including the standard one; because the prompt does not identify the baseline beyond the audience label, a flag on the standard response is not excluded by construction and is treated as noise.

\section[\appendixname~\thesection]{Illustrative Examples of Dangerous Analogy Patterns}
\label{appendix:analogies}

The examples illustrate the patterns the codebook targets; they are not verbatim corpus quotations and are not tied to adjudication outcomes.

\textbf{Example 1: Semaphore as traffic light.} Green means go, red means stop. This captures binary blocking but omits counting semantics (a semaphore can admit several concurrent accesses) and the atomicity of wait and signal. A student may conclude that a semaphore is a boolean flag and not see how it differs from a mutex.

\textbf{Example 2: Deadlock as two people in a doorway.} This captures mutual waiting but omits necessary conditions: resource holding, no preemption, and circular wait. A student may believe any two mutually waiting processes are deadlocked.

\textbf{Example 3: Database index as book index.} This captures the read-speed benefit but omits write overhead and the selectivity requirement; an index on a low-selectivity column can be slower than a full scan. A student may believe an index always speeds up queries.

\textbf{Example 4: TCP handshake as a greeting.} ``Hello?'' ``Hello, I hear you.'' ``Great, let's talk.'' This captures the message exchange but omits the purpose (synchronising sequence numbers, establishing parameters). A student may believe the handshake is a mere formality.

\textbf{Example 5: Garbage collection as cleaning up.} This captures the outcome but omits the mechanism (reachability analysis; mark-and-sweep vs.\ reference counting), possible pauses, and that reference counting cannot collect cycles. A student may believe garbage collection is a free background process.
